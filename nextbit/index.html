01 / The business problem

Finding equipment
is only half the job.

An interview shoot needs a working setup, not just a camera.

In the existing journey, renters had to browse individual items, compare specifications, assemble a setup, and work out dates and pickup. That places the burden of coordination on the person trying to plan a production.

For indie filmmakers, students, small crews, and content creators, those decisions sit alongside tight budgets and production schedules. The design opportunity was to make a suitable starting point easier to find, while keeping room for more specific equipment choices.

The insight that shaped the work

“What am I shooting?”
comes before
“Which gear do I need?”

This framing emerged from team discussions and the field observations documented in my project deck. It became the organizing principle for the booking experience.

02 / A product decision

What should a kit
be organized around?

I compared three ways to help renters find a starting point. Each made a different part of the decision easier.

A

Rental duration

One day / Weekend / Three days

Useful for planning time, but it does not tell a renter which equipment belongs together.

B

Equipment category

Camera / Lighting / Audio

Familiar to experienced renters, but still asks them to build a complete setup from separate parts.

C Selected direction

Shoot purpose

Interview / Short film / Creator

Connects a production goal to a ready-made starting point, with equipment choices available inside the kit.

The tradeoff: a kit cannot anticipate every production. I kept category browsing and equipment swaps available so the shortcut would not become a constraint.

A change in the sequence of decisions

From

Browse gear → Compare specs → Build a setup → Work out dates

To

Choose a shoot → Review a kit → Adjust equipment → Select dates → Reserve

03 / The designed experience

Three moments.
Less uncertainty.

01

A useful place
to begin.

The home screen asks “What are you shooting today?” and introduces kits around production needs. An Interview Kit makes the purpose legible before the user has to evaluate individual specifications.

Search and category browsing remain available for renters who already know what they want.

Design intent / Make the first choice feel manageable.

02

A starting point,
with room to adjust.

The kit detail screen brings the included equipment into view. Categories such as camera, lenses, audio, and lighting help renters understand the setup without losing the context of the kit.

Equipment swaps preserve flexibility: the suggested setup supports a decision without making every decision for the renter.

Design intent / Balance guidance with control.

03

Bring the plan
together before booking.

Reservation review gathers the selected kit, additional equipment, and rental details into one place. Date selection is part of the same flow, followed by a reservation state users can return to.

The aim is to make the commitment explicit: what is being rented, and when.

Design intent / Keep logistics connected to the equipment decision.

Looking ahead

Confidence before
commitment.

The same idea extends beyond equipment kits. My additional concepts explored guided robotics studio sessions and equipment demonstrations as ways to understand an unfamiliar tool before reserving it.

For the core booking flow, a next validation step would be to compare kit-based and individual-item tasks with the same shoot brief, tracking completion time, equipment errors, and confidence. That would help separate the value of the design from broader business changes.
